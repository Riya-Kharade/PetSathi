Small Paws,
Thoughtful Care.

Healthy rabbits need space to hop, a hay-rich diet, gentle handling, regular grooming, and careful health checks. Build a safe, comfortable routine with this practical guide.

01 • EVERYDAY ROUTINE

Daily Rabbit Care

Rabbits can hide discomfort, so a calm daily check matters. Keep food and water fresh, keep their toilet area clean, and learn what your rabbit's normal appetite, behaviour, and droppings look like.

🌾

Unlimited Good-Quality Hay

Keep fresh grass hay available throughout the day. Hay supplies fibre for digestive function and helps wear down continuously growing teeth. Choose clean, dry hay without a musty or mouldy smell.

💧

Fresh Drinking Water

Provide clean water every day in a stable bowl or a suitable bottle. Wash and refill the container daily, and check that a bottle's spout is not blocked.

🥬

Balanced, Rabbit-Safe Food

Offer suitable leafy greens and a measured amount of plain rabbit pellets alongside hay. Introduce new foods gradually. Fruit and other sugary treats should be limited, not used as main food.

🧹

Clean the Litter Area

Remove wet or soiled litter and leftover fresh food. Wash bowls and refresh bedding as needed. Clean surroundings make it easier to notice changes in urine and droppings.

🔎

Observe Appetite and Droppings

Check that your rabbit is eating, drinking, moving, and passing its usual dry droppings. A sudden reduction or stop in eating or droppings needs prompt veterinary advice.

🧸

Movement and Enrichment

Provide daily opportunities to hop, explore, forage, chew safe materials, and retreat to a hiding place. Keep exercise time supervised in a rabbit-proofed area.

🤲

Gentle, Safe Handling

Interact at floor level when possible. If lifting is necessary, support the chest and hindquarters securely and keep your rabbit close to your body. Never lift by the ears.

02 • A SAFE PLACE TO HOP

Housing & Environment

A rabbit's home should be spacious, well ventilated, quiet, and designed for normal rabbit behaviour: hopping, stretching, hiding, digging, chewing, and resting.

📏

Room to Move

Choose a secure exercise pen or enclosure that lets your rabbit stand fully upright, stretch out, and take several full hops in every direction. Bigger is better, especially when free-roam time is limited.

🛏️

Comfortable Resting Space

Provide a dry, soft resting area and at least one hideout where your rabbit can retreat. Use safe, non-slip flooring rather than bare wire floors that can injure feet.

🚽

Suitable Litter Box

Use a roomy litter tray with rabbit-safe paper-based litter or another vet-approved substrate. Keep it clean and place hay nearby to encourage appropriate toilet habits.

🌬️

Good Airflow and Comfort

Keep the enclosure dry and well ventilated, away from direct sun, draughts, smoke, and strong chemical smells. Rabbits can overheat, so watch the room temperature carefully in warm weather.

🧩

Enrichment and Companionship

Offer safe tunnels, cardboard hideouts, forage activities, and chew toys. Rabbits are social; discuss suitable companionship and gradual bonding with a rabbit-savvy professional.

🧽

Clean, Dry Surfaces

Remove soiled litter and wet bedding frequently. Clean the enclosure with rabbit-safe products, rinse where appropriate, and let surfaces dry before your rabbit returns.

🏠 Indoor Housing

Indoor housing makes it easier to monitor eating, droppings, and behaviour, while protecting rabbits from many outdoor hazards.

  • Use a secure pen or rabbit-proofed room with non-slip flooring.
  • Cover electrical cables and block access to unsafe furniture or plants.
  • Provide shade, ventilation, a hideout, and supervised exercise space.
  • Keep the area calm and away from loud noise and cooking fumes.

🌳 Outdoor Time and Housing

Outdoor access needs careful supervision and protection. A simple hutch alone is not enough space or protection for a rabbit.

  • Use a secure, predator-resistant run with shelter from sun, rain, and wind.
  • Provide shade and constant access to fresh water; avoid heat and temperature extremes.
  • Check for gaps, toxic plants, pesticides, parasites, and contact with wild rabbits.
  • Bring the rabbit to a safe indoor area when conditions are unsuitable.
🏡🐰
A HAPPY HABITAT

A home should support rabbit behaviour.

Think beyond a cage: give your rabbit room to move, a clean toilet area, a comfortable hideout, safe things to chew, and a calm place to rest. Check the setup regularly for wear, sharp edges, and escape routes.

03 • GENTLE, REGULAR MAINTENANCE

Grooming & Hygiene

Grooming helps you keep the coat clean and spot skin changes, matting, and parasites. The right routine depends on coat length, shedding, age, and your rabbit's health.

🪮

Brush the Coat

Brush short-coated rabbits regularly and increase brushing during heavy shedding. Long-haired rabbits may need daily grooming to prevent painful mats. Use a gentle tool and stop if your rabbit becomes distressed.

✂️

Check and Trim Nails

Check nail length often. Ask a rabbit-experienced vet or trained groomer to show you safe trimming technique, especially if the nails are dark or your rabbit struggles.

🧴

Keep the Coat Dry

Rabbits generally should not be given routine full-body baths. Wet fur can be stressful and difficult to dry safely. Seek veterinary advice if the coat is soiled or your rabbit cannot groom itself.

🐾

Check Feet and Skin

Look for sore areas, redness, hair loss, scabs, swelling, or damp fur around the feet and underside. A rabbit that is overweight or has mobility problems may need extra help staying clean.

👂

Eyes, Ears, and Nose

Observe for discharge, crusting, redness, persistent scratching, or head shaking. Do not put cotton buds, oils, or unprescribed drops into the ears or eyes.

🧺

Clean Bedding and Litter

Remove damp litter and soiled bedding, wash food and water containers, and keep resting areas dry. Never use strongly scented cleaners or products not confirmed safe for rabbits.

⚠️
Never pull at tight mats or cut close to the skin

Rabbit skin is delicate. Severe matting, dirty or wet fur around the rear, or a rabbit that cannot groom itself should be assessed by a veterinarian or a suitably experienced rabbit groomer.

04 • NOTICE THE LITTLE CHANGES

Health & Wellness

Rabbits often hide signs of pain. A change in appetite, droppings, posture, movement, or grooming can be important. Get to know your rabbit's normal patterns and contact a veterinarian when something changes.

🦷
Dental problems
Overgrown or misaligned teeth may cause drooling, a wet chin, difficulty eating, or weight loss.
🍽️
Digestive slowdown
Reduced appetite or fewer droppings can signal a serious gut problem that needs urgent attention.
🪰
Flystrike risk
Wet or soiled fur around the rear can attract flies. Check this area daily, especially in warm weather.
👂
Ear and skin concerns
Crusts, redness, itching, hair loss, or head shaking may need veterinary assessment.
🐾
Sore feet or mobility changes
Limping, reluctance to move, or sore patches on the feet should not be ignored.
⚖️
Weight or behaviour changes
Unexpected weight change, hiding, unusual quietness, or a hunched posture can be warning signs.

🩺 Regular Veterinary Checkups

Arrange routine examinations with a veterinarian who is comfortable treating rabbits. Ask for advice on body condition, teeth, nails, diet, parasite prevention, neutering, and how often follow-up visits are appropriate for your rabbit.

🚨 When to seek urgent help

Contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian immediately if you notice any of the following. Do not wait overnight or try home remedies first.

  • Not eating, eating much less, or a sudden stop in droppings.
  • Diarrhoea, a swollen or painful-looking abdomen, or severe weakness.
  • Difficulty breathing, collapse, seizures, or loss of balance.
  • Head tilt, severe bleeding, major injury, or suspected poisoning.
  • Straining to urinate or no urine being passed.
🦷
Dental health starts with the right diet

Rabbit teeth grow continuously. A hay-rich, high-fibre diet supports normal chewing and tooth wear. Chew toys do not replace hay or a veterinary dental examination. Never try to file or trim teeth at home.

Vaccination is location-specific. Ask a rabbit-experienced veterinarian whether vaccines for rabbit haemorrhagic disease (RHD/RHDV) or myxomatosis are relevant where you live, and whether an appropriate product is authorised and available. Disease risk, vaccine availability, and schedules differ between countries and regions. Do not copy a schedule from another country or administer a vaccine without veterinary advice.
Important: This guide is for general education and is not a diagnosis or a treatment plan. Rabbits can deteriorate quickly when they stop eating or passing droppings. A qualified veterinarian must assess symptoms and recommend treatment for the individual animal.
05 • A RABBIT-SAFE HOME

Everyday Safety Tips

Rabbit-proof the spaces your pet can reach. Many hazards are ordinary household items that become dangerous when chewed, swallowed, or encountered without supervision.

🚫 Common household hazards

Electrical cables and chargers

Cover, route, or block access to wires. Chewing an electric cable can cause burns, shock, or serious injury.

Toxic houseplants and garden chemicals

Keep unknown plants, fertilisers, pesticides, weed killers, and slug pellets out of reach. Check plant safety before allowing access.

Unsafe foods and treats

Keep chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and unfamiliar human foods away. Avoid sudden diet changes and large amounts of sugary treats.

Heat, direct sun, and poor ventilation

Provide shade, airflow, and a cool resting area. Never leave a rabbit in a parked vehicle, sunroom, or hot balcony.

Open balconies, windows, and stairs

Block falls and escape routes with secure barriers. Supervise access to steps, ledges, and slippery floors.

Other pets and predators

Never assume a dog or cat will be safe with a rabbit. Use secure separation and supervise any carefully managed introductions.

Small objects and loose materials

Remove plastic, string, foam, fabric, and small items that could be chewed or swallowed. Offer rabbit-safe enrichment instead.

Harsh cleaners and strong fragrances

Keep rabbits away while cleaning. Avoid strong fumes and use only products confirmed safe for the animal and its enclosure.

🤲

Handle with Support

Interact at floor level whenever possible. If lifting is necessary, support the chest and hindquarters securely and keep the rabbit close to your body. Never lift by the ears or hold a rabbit on its back.

🐕

Give Space from Other Animals

Rabbits are prey animals and can be frightened by chasing or sudden approaches. Provide a secure retreat and never leave a rabbit alone with a dog, cat, or other pet.

📞

Prepare for Emergencies

Save the phone number and address of a rabbit-experienced clinic. Keep a secure carrier ready and know the quickest safe route for urgent veterinary care.

💚
Let your rabbit choose contact

Many rabbits feel safer when people sit quietly at their level and allow them to approach. If a rabbit struggles or appears frightened during handling, lower it safely and give it space.

QUICK ANSWERS

Rabbit Care FAQs

Practical answers to common questions from rabbit owners.

What should make up most of a rabbit's diet?

For most adult rabbits, good-quality grass hay should make up the largest part of the diet and be available at all times. Fresh water, suitable leafy greens, and a measured amount of plain pellets complete the routine. Ask your vet about the right diet for young, senior, or unwell rabbits.

Can I keep my rabbit in a small cage all day?

No. Rabbits need enough room to stand upright, stretch, hop, explore, and exercise every day. A secure pen or rabbit-proofed room with a resting hideout is more suitable than long-term confinement in a small cage.

How often should I groom my rabbit?

It depends on coat type and shedding. Short-haired rabbits need regular brushing, while long-haired rabbits often need daily coat care to prevent mats. Ask a vet or experienced rabbit groomer to demonstrate a safe routine.

Should rabbits be bathed?

Routine full-body bathing is generally not recommended. If your rabbit is dirty, has a soiled rear, or cannot groom itself, ask a veterinarian for safe, case-specific help rather than attempting a full bath.

What if my rabbit stops eating or producing droppings?

Treat this as urgent. Contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian immediately. Do not wait to see whether the rabbit improves, and do not give human medicines or home remedies unless a vet instructs you.

Do rabbits need vaccinations?

Recommendations depend on local disease risks and which vaccines are authorised and available in your region. Ask a rabbit-experienced veterinarian about RHD/RHDV and myxomatosis rather than relying on a generic schedule from another country.
