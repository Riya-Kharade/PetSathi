01 • THE EVERYDAY MENU

Build a Balanced Rabbit Diet

Rabbits are grazing herbivores. A high-fibre menu supports normal digestion and helps wear down teeth that grow throughout life. Hay and grass should be the foundation—not a side dish.

A simple way to picture the plate

Use this approximate breakdown as a learning guide for a healthy adult rabbit. The exact amount of greens and pellets should be adjusted for age, weight, health and the advice of a rabbit-savvy veterinarian.

Remember: Keep good-quality feeding hay available at all times. The “85 / 10 / 5” split is a broad guide, not a weighing formula for every rabbit.
85%
Hay & grass Unlimited access to fresh, clean feeding hay and safe grass.
10%
Leafy greens A varied selection of washed greens, herbs and suitable vegetables.
5%
Pellets / nuggets A small, measured amount of suitable high-fibre rabbit pellets.

The foundation

Hay & fresh grass

Offer unlimited clean feeding hay every day. Safe fresh grass can add variety; introduce it gradually if your rabbit is not used to it.

Fresh variety

Leafy vegetables & herbs

Offer a varied handful of rabbit-safe greens. Try romaine, rocket, spring greens, watercress, coriander, basil or mint.

Wash well • Rotate varieties • Introduce slowly

Measured portion

Rabbit pellets / nuggets

Choose plain, high-fibre rabbit pellets and measure the daily ration. Avoid colourful muesli-style mixes that encourage selective feeding.

Small supplement—not a hay replacement

Always available

Fresh drinking water

Keep clean water available all day and refresh it daily. Many rabbits find a stable, heavy water bowl easier to drink from than a bottle.

Check the bowl and refill whenever needed

Tiny treat

Fruit & root vegetables

Small pieces of fruit or carrot can be an occasional treat only. They contain more sugar than leafy greens; carrot tops are a better regular green option.

Keep treats small and infrequent

Mix it up

Herbs for variety

Fresh basil, coriander, dill, mint, parsley and thyme can add variety. Wash them, offer small amounts at first, and observe your rabbit.

Use pesticide-free, correctly identified plants
02 • FRESH & RABBIT-FRIENDLY

Foods That Can Be Safe

Variety matters, but not every vegetable belongs in the same portion. Choose familiar, rabbit-safe plants and introduce each new food slowly.

Photo: Jennifer Braun / Pexels ↗
Leafy greens

Romaine & spring greens

Offer washed leaves as part of a varied daily green selection. Rotate with other suitable greens rather than relying on one type.

Photo: Pexels photographer / Pexels ↗
Leafy greens

Rocket & watercress

These are listed among suitable greens for rabbits. Wash thoroughly and combine with other safe leaves for variety.

Photo: Anna Tarazevich / Pexels ↗
Vegetable

Bell peppers

Red, yellow and green bell peppers are on PDSA's rabbit-safe vegetable list. Remove the stem and seeds, wash, and serve a suitable amount.

Photo: Polina Tankilevitch / Pexels ↗
Fresh herbs

Coriander & basil

Fragrant herbs can make greens more interesting. Offer clean, fresh leaves in small varied portions.

Photo: alleksana / Pexels ↗
Fresh herbs

Mint, dill & parsley

PDSA lists these herbs as suitable options. Rotate them with other greens; parsley and some other plants should be portioned sensibly.

Photo: Heather Brock / Pexels ↗
Occasional treat

Fruit in tiny portions

Small pieces of apple without pips, strawberry, pear or banana may be occasional treats. Fruit is sugary and should not be a daily staple.

🧼
Prepare fresh food carefully

Wash produce, remove spoiled leaves and only offer plants you can confidently identify. Introduce new greens one at a time and gradually; stop and seek veterinary advice if your rabbit develops digestive changes.

03 • KEEP THESE OUT OF THE BOWL

Foods to Avoid

Some foods can be toxic, while others are too rich in sugar or starch for a rabbit's digestive system. When you cannot identify a plant or ingredient with confidence, do not feed it.

🥣

Skip the colourful muesli mix

Rabbits may pick out the sweeter, starchier pieces and leave the fibrous parts. PDSA and RSPCA recommend a simple, measured high-fibre pellet or nugget rather than muesli-style mixes. Do not replace hay with pellets.

04 • MAKE IT A ROUTINE

Simple Feeding Guidelines

A consistent routine makes it easier to notice changes in appetite and keep portions balanced. Rabbits naturally graze throughout the day.

Morning

Refresh & refill

Top up fresh feeding hay, replace drinking water, offer a varied portion of washed greens and measure the day's pellet ration.

Throughout the day

Keep grazing available

Leave good-quality hay available at all times. Check that the hay stays clean and dry, and watch that your rabbit is eating normally.

Evening

Offer greens & observe

Offer the remaining planned greens if feeding twice daily, refresh water and notice appetite, droppings and general behaviour.

🥄 Portion awareness

PDSA's 2026 PAW Report describes approximately 15 g of commercial rabbit nuggets or pellets per kg of body weight per day, alongside constant access to hay or grass and fresh greens. PDSA's feeding guide also describes a tablespoon-based approach. These are general reference points—not a prescription for every rabbit. Pellet density, age, body condition, activity and medical needs can change the right amount.

Practical rule: Measure pellets rather than topping up a bowl. Ask a rabbit-experienced vet to set the right portion for your rabbit, especially for young, senior, pregnant, overweight or unwell rabbits.

05 • LITTLE HABITS, BIG IMPACT

Everyday Nutrition Tips

Healthy feeding is about consistency and observation—not adding more treats or constantly changing the menu.

🌾

Make hay the priority

Keep fresh, good-quality feeding hay available at all times. Its fibre supports gut movement and chewing helps wear down continuously growing teeth.

⚖️

Watch body condition

Weigh your rabbit regularly and monitor body shape. Avoid adding extra pellets and treats without accounting for them in the daily ration.

🔄

Change food gradually

Introduce new greens or a new pellet food slowly. PDSA advises gradual transitions over around 2–4 weeks when changing a rabbit's diet.

🧺

Make feeding enriching

Scatter a small measured pellet ration in a clean area or hide suitable greens among hay so your rabbit can forage and stay engaged.

🚰

Keep water clean

Provide constant access to fresh water. Check bowls daily, clean them regularly and refill whenever water becomes dirty or low.

🥬

Keep treats occasional

Use a tiny piece of fruit or carrot only occasionally. For frequent rewards, use part of the rabbit's planned greens or pellet allowance.

🚨
If your rabbit stops eating, contact a vet immediately.

A rabbit that is not eating or producing droppings may have a serious problem such as gut stasis. Do not wait for the appetite to return on its own or try to treat it at home.

QUICK ANSWERS

Rabbit Nutrition FAQs

Simple answers to common feeding questions.

Can rabbits eat carrots every day?

Carrot root is relatively sugary and should be offered only as a small occasional treat. Carrot tops (the leafy greens) can be part of a varied selection of rabbit-safe greens.

Is hay really that important?

Yes. Hay and grass should make up most of a rabbit's diet. Their fibre supports normal gut movement, and chewing helps wear down teeth that continue to grow throughout life.

Can I give my rabbit a new vegetable straight away?

Introduce unfamiliar greens gradually and one at a time. A sudden change can upset the digestive system. Stop the new food and contact a vet if you notice reduced appetite or abnormal droppings.

Are store-bought rabbit treats healthy?

Many commercial treats are high in sugar. A tiny amount of suitable fresh fruit, vegetables or herbs is usually a better occasional treat. Avoid yoghurt drops, sweets and cereal-based snacks.

What if my rabbit refuses food?

Contact a rabbit-experienced veterinarian immediately if your rabbit stops eating. Reduced or absent eating can quickly become an emergency in rabbits.
